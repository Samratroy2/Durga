The Bonedi Bari

The House
& The Family

Eight generations, one courtyard, and a pujo that has never missed a year.

History

Why the pujo began

Ram Kamal Roy built this house in 1798 on the profits of the indigo and salt trade, on a plot of land facing the Hooghly.

Family record holds that the first pujo, in 1801, was a thanksgiving — Ram Kamal had lost a cargo ship in a storm the year before, and vowed to hold a Durga pujo every year the family survived in trade. The vow outlived the trade by two centuries.

What began as a private, family-only affair grew as the household grew: by the 1870s, the pujo was drawing neighbours from across Baghbazar.

Architecture

The dalan and the courtyard

The thakur dalan — the columned hall where the idol stands — faces east, so the morning sun reaches the Goddess's face first.

The courtyard's red-oxide floor, relaid in the 1985 restoration using the original 1798 technique, is where Sindoor Khela and the family's evening arati both take place.

A wrought-iron balustrade, added around 1890, rings the upper gallery from which the women of earlier generations watched the rituals below.

Two rooms beyond the dalan hold the family's pujo-khata ledgers, glass-plate negatives, and the silver panchapradip lamp used every Sandhi pujo since 1905.

Family story

Interesting stories and legends

The ship that came back

Family legend holds that the cargo ship believed lost in the 1800 storm returned to port eleven months later, its crew intact — three weeks before the first pujo was held.

The silent year

In 1857, the pujo was held with no dhak and no invited guests. The only surviving record is a single line in the ledger: the date, and the priest's name.

The pomegranate branch

The Nabapatrika bundle used every Saptami includes a pomegranate branch grown in the courtyard itself — from a tree family lore says Ram Kamal planted with his own hands.
